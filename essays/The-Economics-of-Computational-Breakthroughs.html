The Economics of Computational Breakthroughs

Why solving the impossible does not necessarily make it affordable.

A Mathematical Breakthrough That May Change Almost Nothing

In October 2026, OpenAI published a paper with an extraordinary title: The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False.

The paper proposed an algorithm that, if its proof survives independent scrutiny, would overturn one of the most important assumptions in theoretical computer science.

For decades, researchers have studied the difficulty of solving certain computational problems as their size increases.

Some problems can be solved efficiently. Others appear to require an enormous number of computational steps.

The Exponential Time Hypothesis, or ETH, suggests that certain problems, particularly Boolean satisfiability problems such as 3-SAT, cannot be solved in subexponential time in the worst case.

OpenAI challenged that assumption.

Its proposed algorithm has a running-time bound of:

poly(n,m) × 2^O(n log log n / √log n)

For sufficiently large inputs, this grows more slowly than any fixed exponential function of the form 2^(cn), where c is a positive constant.

Mathematically, that is an extraordinary claim.

It does not prove that P equals NP.

It does not make every computationally difficult problem easy.

But if correct, it changes an important boundary in our understanding of computation.

And yet, something even more interesting happens when we examine the economics.

The algorithm may be mathematically revolutionary while remaining economically useless.

An independent analysis by Fabio Dias and Yvens Lima examined the practical implications of the claimed result.

Their analysis showed that, depending on the constants hidden inside the mathematical notation, the theoretical upper bound may not outperform brute-force search until the number of variables reaches astronomical levels.

With an illustrative hidden constant of 2, the crossover occurs around 10^32 variables.

With a constant of 3, it moves to approximately 10^126.

With a constant of 10, it reaches roughly 10^3517.

These are not measurements of the algorithm's actual runtime. They are conditional estimates of when its theoretical bound becomes smaller than a brute-force benchmark.

Nevertheless, they reveal a fundamental problem.

A breakthrough can transform the mathematics of computation without transforming the economics of computation.

A problem can become theoretically easier while remaining practically impossible.

The boundary of mathematical possibility can move without moving the boundary of commercial feasibility.

This distinction deserves far more attention.

Because the future of artificial intelligence, scientific discovery, industrial technology, and economic competition will depend not only on which problems we can solve.

It will depend on which problems we can afford to solve.

The Difference Between Possible and Affordable

Classical computational complexity asks how the resources required to solve a problem grow as the problem becomes larger.

It is concerned with the mathematical structure of computation.

An algorithm may require polynomial time.

Another may require exponential time.

A third may achieve a better asymptotic bound without becoming practical at any relevant input size.

These distinctions are essential.

They tell us what computation can theoretically achieve.

But economies do not operate in asymptotic space.

Businesses do not purchase mathematical elegance.

Governments do not build infrastructure around theoretical upper bounds.

Investors do not allocate capital based solely on whether an exponent has improved.

They operate within physical and financial constraints.

They have budgets.

They have deadlines.

They have limited electricity.

They have limited hardware.

They have limited engineering talent.

They face competitors, regulation, supply-chain dependencies, and uncertainty.

For them, the important question is not simply whether a problem can be solved.

It is whether the problem can be solved at a cost that makes the result valuable.

Consider two algorithms.

The first has a better theoretical growth rate but requires expensive infrastructure, specialised expertise, and enormous fixed costs.

The second has a worse theoretical growth rate but runs efficiently on ordinary hardware for the input sizes that actually matter.

Mathematics may favour the first algorithm in the limit.

Economics may favour the second for decades.

Or perhaps forever within the relevant commercial market.

This creates a distinction between two frontiers.

The frontier of mathematical solvability.

And the frontier of economic solvability.

They are related.

But they are not the same.

A Model of Effective Computational Complexity

This distinction is the foundation of what I call the Economic Model of Computational Complexity.

The central proposal is simple.

The practical difficulty of a computational problem is determined by more than its mathematical structure.

It is also determined by the economic conditions under which the problem is solved and the strategic behaviour of the actors competing to solve it.

Conceptually:

Ce = f(M, E, S)

Where:

Ce represents effective computational complexity.

M represents mathematical complexity.

E represents economic complexity.

S represents strategic complexity.

Mathematical complexity describes the formal computational requirements of the problem.

Economic complexity describes the resources and costs involved in producing a useful solution.

Strategic complexity describes how competition, cooperation, incentives, and control over critical resources change those costs.

This is a conceptual framework, not a replacement for classical complexity theory.

It does not claim that electricity prices alter whether a problem belongs to P or NP.

It argues that formal complexity alone cannot determine the economic feasibility of computation.

And the OpenAI case illustrates why.

If the proposed algorithm is correct, the mathematical frontier has moved.

But the economic frontier may remain almost exactly where it was.

The breakthrough changes what is possible in principle.

It may not change what is affordable in practice.

That difference is where economic analysis becomes necessary.

The Hidden Economics Inside an Algorithm

The conventional presentation of an algorithm often focuses on its asymptotic running time.

But asymptotic notation hides important information.

It can hide constants.

It can hide implementation costs.

It can hide memory requirements.

It can hide communication overhead.

It can hide the cost of specialised hardware.

It can hide the cost of developing, testing, and maintaining the system.

A mathematically superior algorithm can therefore be economically inferior.

To make this distinction measurable, effective computational cost can be represented as:

C_eff,i(n,t) = min [K_i(a,t) + p_i(t)R_a(n,t) + V_iT_a(n,t)]

The minimum is taken over the algorithms available to organisation i at time t that satisfy the required quality and delivery constraints.

Where:

K represents the cost of implementing and integrating an algorithm.

p represents the effective price of computational resources.

R represents the quantity of resources consumed.

T represents the time required to produce the result.

V represents the economic cost of time.

This is a proposed economic cost model, not an established theorem of computational complexity.

Its purpose is to make a distinction visible.

Two organisations can face the same mathematical problem while experiencing entirely different effective costs.

One may have access to specialised accelerators.

Another may depend on expensive cloud infrastructure.

One may own proprietary datasets.

Another may spend years acquiring and cleaning data.

One may possess the technical knowledge to implement a new algorithm.

Another may know that the algorithm exists but lack the capability to use it.

One may secure inexpensive energy.

Another may face grid constraints that prevent expansion.

The mathematical problem may be identical.

The effective cost is not.

And in competitive markets, that difference can be more important than the algorithm itself.

Artificial Intelligence Is Changing the Cost of Discovery

There is another dimension to the OpenAI announcement.

The breakthrough was reportedly generated by an AI system.

That introduces a second economic question.

What happens when the cost of discovering new algorithms begins to decline?

Historically, advances in theoretical computer science depended on highly specialised human researchers.

Their work required years of education.

Years of investigation.

Institutional support.

Research funding.

And often long periods of uncertainty.

Now imagine an environment in which AI systems can help researchers generate new mathematical arguments, explore possible proofs, formalise results, and identify relationships that would otherwise require enormous human effort.

The marginal cost of generating candidate discoveries could fall.

The number of candidate discoveries could increase.

The speed of mathematical exploration could accelerate.

But the cost of verifying those discoveries may not fall at the same rate.

A system capable of generating hundreds of mathematical claims creates a new economic burden if each claim requires substantial expert review.

The cost of producing a proposed solution may decline while the cost of establishing its correctness remains high.

This creates another important distinction.

Discovery is not verification.

And verification is not necessarily cheap simply because discovery has become cheap.

A formal proof assistant can help.

But formal verification still depends on what has actually been encoded and proved.

In OpenAI's case, the published Lean formalisation establishes a weaker asymptotic bound than the headline result in the accompanying paper.

Both bounds would be sufficient to challenge ETH if correctly established.

But the distinction matters.

The economic value of AI-generated discoveries depends not only on how quickly they are produced.

It depends on how reliably they can be verified, reproduced, understood, and converted into practical applications.

The bottleneck may move from discovery to verification.

From verification to implementation.

From implementation to infrastructure.

And from infrastructure to commercial adoption.

Every time one constraint disappears, another may become decisive.

The Strategic Game Behind Computation

Computational problems are not solved in isolation.

They are solved by organisations competing for resources, capabilities, customers, and economic advantage.

Consider two AI laboratories.

Both have access to similar mathematical knowledge.

Both understand the same algorithms.

Both can read the same published research.

Yet one may have substantially lower costs than the other.

Why?

It may have better infrastructure.

It may have cheaper electricity.

It may have more efficient software.

It may have stronger relationships with semiconductor manufacturers.

It may have greater access to capital.

It may possess proprietary data.

It may have developed better internal tools for evaluating new discoveries.

Its advantage does not necessarily come from knowing mathematics that competitors do not know.

Its advantage may come from turning the same mathematics into useful results at lower cost.

This is where game theory becomes important.

Companies do not simply compete to solve computational problems.

They compete to shape the economic conditions under which those problems are solved.

One company invests in specialised chips.

Another develops more efficient algorithms.

One secures long-term energy contracts.

Another improves model compression.

One acquires proprietary data.

Another builds an open-source ecosystem.

One controls distribution.

Another reduces the price of inference.

Every strategic decision changes the effective cost structure of computation.

Sometimes for one company.

Sometimes for an entire industry.

And sometimes for competitors who never participated in the original decision.

This makes computational economics dynamic.

The cost of solving a problem is not merely determined by technology.

It is partly determined by the strategic behaviour of the organisations that control technology.

Relative Computational Advantage

This leads to what I consider the most important implication of the framework.

Companies do not necessarily need to solve the hardest problems in the world.

They need to solve commercially important problems more efficiently than their competitors.

I call this Relative Computational Advantage.

An organisation possesses relative computational advantage when its effective cost of producing a comparable computational result is lower than that of its competitors.

One possible representation is:

RCA_i = C_benchmark / C_eff,i

An RCA greater than one indicates a cost advantage relative to the chosen benchmark.

This formulation assumes comparable outputs, reliability, and service quality.

The advantage can emerge from many sources.

Better algorithms.

Cheaper energy.

More efficient hardware.

Superior data.

Greater engineering productivity.

Better organisational coordination.

Lower capital costs.

Stronger infrastructure.

Faster deployment.

Or privileged access to an essential input.

Consider NVIDIA.

Its competitive position cannot be explained by semiconductor performance alone.

Its software ecosystem reduces development effort.

Its networking technologies address communication bottlenecks.

Its integrated systems reduce deployment friction.

Its developer ecosystem makes specialised computing more accessible.

These capabilities can lower the effective cost of computation for customers.

At the same time, the ecosystem can increase switching costs and make competition more difficult for rival suppliers.

NVIDIA does not need to change the mathematical complexity of every workload.

It can create economic value by changing the cost of running those workloads.

And it can create strategic advantage by influencing the infrastructure on which other organisations depend.

This is a different form of computational power.

Not the power to solve a problem that nobody else can solve.

But the power to make solving it cheaper, faster, and easier within an ecosystem that others struggle to replicate.

A Mathematical Breakthrough Is Not an Economic Breakthrough

The OpenAI announcement exposes a broader misunderstanding about technological progress.

We tend to treat breakthroughs as singular events.

A problem is solved.

A theory is disproved.

An algorithm is discovered.

A model achieves a new benchmark.

And the world is expected to change.

But economic transformation rarely follows that sequence automatically.

A scientific discovery can exist for decades before becoming commercially useful.

A new algorithm may require hardware that does not yet exist.

A new computational capability may depend on energy infrastructure that has not yet been built.

An AI system may outperform humans in a controlled benchmark while remaining too expensive, unreliable, or difficult to integrate into real operations.

There are several separate transitions.

From mathematical possibility to algorithmic implementation.

From implementation to affordable computation.

From affordable computation to reliable deployment.

From deployment to economically valuable applications.

From valuable applications to durable competitive advantage.

Each transition introduces new constraints.

And each constraint has its own economics.

This is why an improvement in theoretical computational complexity cannot automatically be translated into economic productivity.

A better algorithm may change the mathematical frontier.

Cheaper compute may change the operational frontier.

Better organisations may change the deployment frontier.

And strategic control over resources may determine who captures the value.

These frontiers move at different speeds.

Sometimes together.

Often independently.

The Next Frontier of Computational Economics

The next forty years may produce extraordinary advances in computational capability.

AI systems may discover better algorithms.

Specialised hardware may reduce the cost of computation.

New energy infrastructure may expand available capacity.

Automation may reduce engineering and organisational costs.

Open-source systems may distribute capabilities that once belonged exclusively to large institutions.

Scientific discovery may become increasingly computational.

But none of these developments guarantees that economic advantage will become equally distributed.

In fact, declining computational costs may increase the importance of other scarce resources.

If algorithms become widely available, proprietary data may become more valuable.

If hardware becomes cheaper, access to electricity may become more important.

If AI models become commoditised, distribution and integration may become the principal sources of advantage.

If discovery becomes cheap, verification and institutional trust may become more valuable.

If software becomes easier to build, the ability to deploy it across complex organisations may become the real constraint.

The scarcity does not necessarily disappear.

It moves.

And the organisations that identify the next scarcity before their competitors may capture disproportionate economic value.

This is why understanding computational progress requires more than following model benchmarks or algorithmic discoveries.

It requires understanding the changing relationship between mathematics, infrastructure, capital, institutions, and strategic behaviour.

A Different Question for the AI Economy

The original P versus NP problem asks whether every problem whose solution can be checked efficiently can also be solved efficiently.

The Exponential Time Hypothesis asks a narrower but deeply important question about the time required to solve satisfiability problems.

OpenAI's recent research, if validated, may change the answer to the second question.

But the Economic Model of Computational Complexity asks something different.

Even when a problem becomes mathematically easier, who can afford to solve it?

At what input size?

Using which infrastructure?

At what energy cost?

With what capital requirements?

Within what timeframe?

Who can verify the result?

Who can turn it into a reliable product?

Who controls the resources required?

Who benefits when the cost falls?

And who loses when a previously scarce capability becomes widely available?

These questions are not substitutes for mathematical complexity theory.

They concern the economic consequences of computational capability.

The OpenAI case provides an unusually clear illustration.

A proposed algorithm can overturn a longstanding theoretical assumption while offering no immediate practical advantage.

At the same time, the AI-assisted discovery process may signal a profound change in the economics of producing mathematical knowledge itself.

One frontier may have moved.

Another may not have moved at all.

And a third may be beginning to change much faster than we expected.

Understanding the difference is essential.

Conclusion

For decades, computational progress has largely been understood through the lens of faster processors, better algorithms, and greater computational capacity.

Those measures remain important.

But they are incomplete.

The economic value of computation depends on the cost of converting mathematical capability into useful outcomes.

That cost is shaped by hardware, energy, capital, data, expertise, time, organisational capability, and strategic competition.

A mathematically difficult problem can become economically routine.

A mathematically improved algorithm can remain commercially useless.

A company with superior technology can lose to a competitor with better infrastructure.

And a company with ordinary algorithms can build extraordinary economic power by controlling the conditions under which computation takes place.

The distinction is fundamental.

Mathematical complexity determines how computational requirements scale.

Economic complexity determines what it costs to meet those requirements.

Strategic complexity shapes who can access the necessary resources and who captures the resulting value.

The next generation of economic winners may not be those who discover the most advanced algorithms.

They may be those who identify when mathematical advances become economically useful.

Those who reduce the cost of applying them.

Those who build reusable systems around them.

And those who control the scarce resources required to turn computational possibility into economic reality.

The central question is no longer only whether humanity can solve harder problems.

It is whether we can make those solutions affordable.

And, perhaps more importantly, who gets to decide what affordable means.

Because solving the impossible changes mathematics.

Making the impossible affordable changes the economy.
