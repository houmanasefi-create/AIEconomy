AI and Fair Competition

Read this as a systems note, not as a market forecast and not as institutional theater. The question is where agency, constraint, feedback, and ownership move when artificial intelligence becomes a general purpose layer inside economic life. A timeless system view starts with stocks and flows, bottlenecks and feedback loops, incentives and institutional memory. It does not ask whether AI is good or bad in the abstract. It asks which actors gain leverage, which constraints become binding, which costs are hidden, and which decisions compound across time. This essay starts from a premise argued in the note published today: AI can become a concentration machine if policy treats it as just another software market. The evidence broadly supports the concern. Recent work from the OECD, the Competition and Markets Authority, the Federal Trade Commission, and researchers finds that the key choke points in AI are not only model quality, but access to compute, high-quality data, cloud distribution, and incumbent ecosystems that can bundle AI into already-dominant products. The danger is real, but it is not fate. Concentration in AI is a policy choice if governments allow the bottlenecks to harden. The real market failure The core problem is not that AI is powerful. The core problem is that the most powerful parts of the AI stack are becoming vertically integrated. According to the 2026 AI Index, industry produced more than 90% of notable frontier models in 2025, while the most capable systems have become less transparent about training code, parameter counts, datasets, and training duration. That is a dangerous mix: technical concentration plus declining visibility. The cost side makes the structural risk even clearer. One recent institutional estimate finds that the amortized cost of training the most compute-intensive frontier models has risen about 2.4 times per year since 2016 and could exceed $1 billion for the largest training runs by 2027 if the trend continues. NBER work on AI market structure argues that market power in AI will turn on access to data, compute, and distribution, not simply on cleverer algorithms. Put plainly, the decisive competition issue is control over inputs and routes to market. But the evidence does not justify fatalism. The 2025 AI Index showed that leading open-weight models sharply narrowed the performance gap with closed-weight models, and OECD work finds that AI can lower minimum efficient scale and open room for entrants in downstream markets. That matters because it means barriers are not purely technical. They become durable only when policy lets infrastructure control, restrictive licensing, switching costs, and self-preferencing freeze the market. Australia should regulate to preserve contestability, not to ratify incumbency. What the major economies are teaching us Using April 2026 data from the International Monetary Fund, the four largest national economies by nominal GDP are the United States, China, Germany, and Japan. Because Germany's digital and AI rulebook is increasingly shaped at the level of the European Union, that is the more meaningful European comparator for AI policy. Australia, at roughly $2.12 trillion in nominal GDP, is much smaller. That means it cannot win an arms race on raw scale; it has to win on institutional design, speed, and credibility. The U.S. model is scale first and law second. It relies on voluntary standards from the National Institute of Standards and Technology, sectoral law rather than one comprehensive federal statute, and increasingly aggressive antitrust scrutiny of cloud-lab partnerships. At the same time, the White House's own 2026 framework argues that the country needs national rules to avoid a fragmented state-by-state patchwork. The strength of the U.S. model is obvious: capital depth, research intensity, and fast commercialization. Its weakness is equally obvious: it risks leaving structural concentration to ex post enforcement after the market has already tipped. China treats AI as strategic infrastructure. Its 2017 national AI plan set long-range state objectives; the 2023 Interim Measures for generative AI imposed public-facing content, security, and filing obligations; and Beijing's 2025 rhetoric pushed harder on self-sufficiency in chips, software, and industrial deployment. The strength of this model is coordination. The weakness is that political control narrows openness, reduces room for independent experimentation, and weakens the export appeal of Chinese AI systems outside politically aligned markets. China may avoid laissez-faire concentration, but it does so by accepting a much heavier role for the state in what can be built and said. Europe has the most developed rights-and-rules framework. The AI Act entered into force in August 2024, prohibitions and AI literacy obligations started to apply in February 2025, and general-purpose AI obligations applied from August The DMA, meanwhile, is explicitly designed to make digital markets fairer and more contestable through interoperability and data-access disciplines, while the Commission has also moved to back capacity with AI factories and a €20 billion facility for up to five AI gigafactories within a broader €200 billion AI push. Europe's strength is legal clarity on portability, fair dealing, and gatekeeper conduct. Its weakness is that compliance costs arrive faster than frontier capacity. The bloc knows rules matter; it is only now fully reckoning with the fact that compute matters too. Japan is the most interesting outlier. Its 2025 AI Act is promotional rather than restriction-heavy, its 2026 Business AI Guidelines remain soft-law guidance, and its copyright approach has long been unusually permissive toward text and data mining compared with most Western jurisdictions. The strength of Japan's model is lower friction for experimentation and deployment. The weakness is that light-touch governance does not, by itself, solve compute concentration, cloud leverage, or exclusionary partnerships. For Australia, the lesson is not "copy Japan." The lesson is that pro-innovation regulation is possible without pretending structural market power will fix itself. Australia already has more of the pieces than many people admit: a Voluntary AI Safety Standard, an updated federal policy for responsible AI use in government, a National AI Plan, a proposed digital competition regime for major platforms, a new mandatory merger control regime, and ACCC recommendations to keep monitoring emerging digital technologies. What is missing is coherence. Right now Australia has safety language, procurement language, digital-platform language, and industrial-policy language. It still needs a single competition design for the AI stack. The regulatory design that would actually work Australia should not import one giant AI statute that tries to solve safety, competition, labor, copyright, procurement, and industrial policy all at once. That is how governments create compliance theatre and entry barriers. The durable design is layered. Safety rules should target harmful uses and frontier-risk systems. Competition rules should target chokepoints, discriminatory access, and lock-in. Industrial policy should widen access to compute, data, skills, and export channels. That separation takes the best lessons from the U.S., Europe, Japan, and Australia's own framework without importing their worst habits. For AI labs, the right obligations are proportionate ex ante duties once a provider crosses capability or market-power thresholds. Those duties should include independent evaluations, incident reporting, security testing, documentation of training-data provenance and copyright approach, advance disclosure of major infrastructure and distribution partnerships, and merger-style review of exclusive cloud or preferred-access arrangements. The FTC's partnerships study, the CMA's AI principles, and the EU's rules for general-purpose AI providers all point to the same fact pattern: the serious risk is not size alone, but size combined with control over compute, distribution, and adjacent markets. For enterprises, the central rule should be portability with teeth. A business that builds workflows on top of a model provider should be able to export its prompts, logs, retrieval configurations, evaluation records, and fine-tuning assets it supplied, in usable formats, at commercially realistic cost. There should be no punitive egress fees, no contract terms that discriminate against multi-homing, and no self-preferencing by a dominant cloud, app store, search engine, browser, or productivity suite that privileges its own models over rivals. OECD work on portability and AI infrastructure is clear that switching barriers, restrictive licenses, and leverage across layers are where competition quietly dies. For governments, procurement is competition policy in disguise. Public procurement accounts for roughly 13% of GDP across OECD countries. If the state buys AI badly, it can entrench a handful of vendors for a decade. If it buys well, it can create a market for challengers. The default should be multi-vendor procurement, open standards, mandatory AI impact assessments, audit rights, benchmark disclosure where feasible, exit and transition clauses, and bans on terms that prevent switching. Australia already has evidence that procurement design can widen the field: on the federal digital marketplaces, 51% of registered suppliers are small-to-medium and/or Indigenous-owned businesses, and 53% of overall spend goes to those suppliers. That is not a theory. It is proof that market structure can be designed. Australia should also use its new merger tools like it means it. In AI, the old fiction that "this is only a partnership, not a merger" is too thin. Serial acquisitions, acqui-hires that move core teams, minority stakes with governance rights, long-term compute reservations, preferred-access deals, exclusive distribution clauses, and infrastructure partnerships can entrench power before a conventional market-definition exercise catches up. The new merger control regime and the proposed digital competition regime give Australia a serious legal base. The missing move is to make AI infrastructure, model partnerships, and vertical leverage priority cases from day one. Australia's opening Australia cannot and should not try to become a generic frontier-model superpower by matching U.S. or Chinese spending. The size gap is too large. But smaller economies can still become elite places to build, buy, test, and export applied AI. Australia's likely advantage is not "the biggest model." It is a combination of trusted institutions, strong public-sector digital capability, sector depth in energy and resource systems, and the chance to write rules that are pro-entry instead of pro-incumbent. That is the lane. The institutional foundation is stronger than the doom narrative suggests. The World Bank's 2025 GovTech Maturity Index placed Australia in the global top five, and the OECD's 2025 Digital Government Index ranked Australia second overall. The federal AI-in-government policy now requires agencies to adopt structured governance and risk-based actions, while National AI Centre data shows businesses are seeing productivity and decision-making benefits from AI even as smaller firms still report skills and funding constraints. That combination matters. It means Australia already has a public-sector platform for trustworthy demand and a clear policy reason to stop scale from accruing only to the biggest buyers and the biggest vendors. The lithium question needs precision, not slogans. Geoscience Australia says Australia retained its position as the world's top lithium producer in 2023 with 49% of output. But 2026 USGS data shows larger lithium resources in Argentina and Bolivia, and higher resources than Australia in Chile as well. So Australia is a lithium superpower in production, but not an unquestioned number one on every geological measure. That distinction matters. The real opportunity is not to wave a mineral map and claim AI sovereignty. The real opportunity is to turn critical minerals, renewables, storage, and data-centre policy into a reliable compute base. That matters because the International Energy Agency projects that global data-centre electricity consumption could roughly double to around 945 TWh by 2030, with AI-driven demand rising especially fast. In plain English, lithium matters to AI through energy systems more than through mythology. Minerals do not make an AI nation on their own. Cheap, reliable, low-emissions electricity; grid-scale storage; transmission; predictable approvals; and compute precincts do. Australia's National AI Plan already ties AI opportunity to data centres and broader digital infrastructure, and the government's 2026 expectations for data-centre and AI infrastructure developers explicitly frame social licence and national interest as central. The smart play is to build battery-backed, low-emissions compute corridors and treat them as export infrastructure for the Indo-Pacific, not to chase every layer of the AI stack with subsidies and hope. A twenty-year strategy In the near term, Australia should create an AI competition and contestability code inside, or alongside, the proposed digital competition regime. That code should cover designated AI gatekeepers and critical AI infrastructure operators. It should prohibit discriminatory access, self-preferencing, anti-competitive tying, punitive switching costs, and opaque contract terms that lock public buyers and enterprises into one stack. It should also give the ACCC an explicit monitoring unit for the AI stack and require systematic review of cloud-lab-model deals under the new merger regime. This is not radical. It is the AI version of catching market power before it calcifies. Over the next decade, Australia should widen access to the core inputs of competition. That means a national compute access program for startups, researchers, and scale-ups; open public-interest datasets with strong privacy and governance safeguards; procurement settings that create domestic reference customers; and tax and grant settings that reward genuine R&D, not just AI resale. Public compute should be tied to reciprocity conditions, open standards where safe, and research collaboration. OECD work on AI compute and public AI is right on the big point: if governments want markets that stay open, they cannot leave the foundational infrastructure entirely to private gatekeepers. By the 2030s, Australia should stop talking about "an AI industry" as if it were one thing and instead build export specialisation where it has real advantages. That means AI for mining and resource operations, energy optimisation, grid management, climate adaptation, logistics, government technology, and administrative systems in health and education. The public sector should help by being a demanding first customer, not a captive one. No exclusive decade-long AI contracts. No public data giveaways without public value in return. No outsourcing of institutional capability because foreign hyperscalers offered convenience in quarter one. Australia should be a market where startups can enter, scale-ups can win procurement, enterprises can switch, and foreign investors know the rules are stable and fair. By the 2040s, success should be measured by a short, brutal test. Can an Australian startup access compute without begging a gatekeeper? Can an enterprise switch model providers without rewriting its whole business? Can government buy AI without surrendering sovereignty over data, workflows, and standards? Can regulators see concentration forming before it becomes irreversible? Can Australian firms export trusted applied AI into the Indo-Pacific because buyers trust Australian law, not despite it? If the answer to those questions is yes, Australia will not be an AI renter. It will be an AI exporter. A serious opposing model should stay inside the frame. Experts who disagree with this argument may say that diffusion beats concentration, that open models, cheaper inference, regulatory intervention, and ordinary competitive pressure will stop any single actor from holding durable power. They may also argue that human institutions adapt slowly but persistently, so the system absorbs AI as another capital deepening cycle rather than a civilizational break. That objection is useful because it tests the weakest point of the argument: whether the bottleneck is intelligence itself or the surrounding stack of energy, chips, data, law, trust, and deployment rights. The answer is not settled by slogans. It is settled by watching where marginal control accumulates, who can change the rules without asking permission, and which dependencies become invisible because they work too well.
