P versus NP: Is Complexity Absolute, or Is It Relative?

One of the most famous unsolved questions in computer science is deceptively simple:

If a problem is easy to verify, is it also easy to solve?

This is the P versus NP problem.

For decades, the discussion has focused on algorithms, proofs, computational complexity, and mathematics. But I found myself approaching the problem from a different direction. Not because I think I've solved it, but because I think the way we frame the question deserves exploration.

The Standard View

The traditional explanation is straightforward.

Imagine a difficult Sudoku puzzle.

Finding the correct solution may take hours.

Checking a completed solution takes only a minute.

Computer scientists classify these problems differently.

  • P contains problems that can be solved efficiently.
  • NP contains problems whose proposed solutions can be verified efficiently.

The open question is whether every efficiently verifiable problem can also be solved efficiently.

Most researchers believe the answer is no, but nobody has proved it.

The Assumption Hidden in Plain Sight

As I read more about P versus NP, I kept coming back to one assumption that rarely gets questioned.

Time.

More specifically, the amount of computational resources available at a particular moment in history.

The entire discussion assumes a fixed model of computation. But history suggests that what is considered computationally difficult changes dramatically over time.

Consider something as ordinary as searching a database.

In the 1960s, searching thousands of records required expensive mainframes, punch cards, specialized operators, and significant computational resources.

Today, I can search millions of records on my phone in milliseconds.

The problem didn't change.

The available computational infrastructure did.

That observation made me wonder whether complexity is an absolute property of problems or whether it is, at least in part, a property of the computational civilization solving them.

A Different Way to Think About Complexity

Suppose we stopped thinking about computation as a fixed capability and instead viewed it as something that continuously evolves.

Every generation inherits more compute than the last.

Faster processors.

Cheaper storage.

Massively parallel GPUs.

Cloud computing.

Distributed systems.

Large language models.

Agentic software.

Problems that once required research labs are now solved inside a web browser.

This doesn't prove that P equals NP.

Far from it.

But it does raise a different question.

Perhaps there are two kinds of complexity.

The first is mathematical complexity, which studies how algorithms scale regardless of hardware.

The second is economic complexity, which asks how difficult a problem is given the computational infrastructure available to a civilization at a particular point in time.

Those are not the same question.

Complexity as an Economic Resource

This distinction becomes even more interesting in the age of AI.

Today, the bottleneck is often not algorithms.

It is compute.

Who owns the GPUs?

Who has access to electricity?

Who can afford trillion-token training runs?

Who controls the data centres?

Increasingly, intelligence itself is becoming constrained by infrastructure rather than mathematics alone.

A problem that is theoretically difficult may become economically trivial once enough computational resources are deployed against it.

History is full of examples.

Rendering 3D graphics.

Protein folding.

Speech recognition.

Machine translation.

Image generation.

Each looked impossibly expensive until new computational infrastructure shifted the economics.

The underlying mathematics remained largely unchanged.

The available compute transformed what was practical.

This Does Not Solve P versus NP

To be clear, this observation does not overturn computational complexity theory.

Complexity theory studies asymptotic growth.

If a problem fundamentally requires exponential work, then making computers one billion times faster does not magically turn exponential growth into polynomial growth.

That mathematical distinction remains.

But perhaps that is precisely the point.

P versus NP is asking a theoretical question about algorithms.

I am asking a practical question about civilizations.

As compute becomes cheaper, larger, faster, and more abundant, which problems migrate from "economically impossible" to "economically routine"?

That question may be just as important.

From Algorithms to Civilizations

We often think of intelligence as software.

Increasingly, I suspect intelligence is infrastructure.

Every major leap in computing has expanded the set of problems humans can realistically solve.

Perhaps future generations will view some of today's "hard" problems the same way we view searching a database today.

Not because mathematics changed.

Because civilization did.

Whether or not P equals NP may remain one of mathematics' greatest mysteries.

But there is another question worth asking alongside it.

Not whether a problem is theoretically hard.

But whether complexity itself is relative to the computational resources available to the society attempting to solve it.

That may not be a proof.

But it might be a useful way to think about the future of computation.
